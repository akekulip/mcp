Packet spraying spreads each flow of a machine-learning training job across every spine switch, so a link that silently drops a small fraction of its packets leaves its evidence divided among many paths. A detector that only counts arrivals must separate a shrinking deficit from spray noise, at a cost growing as the inverse square of the loss rate, and one vantage cannot tell an uplink from the downlink behind it. In this paper, we measure what an in-fabric per-directed-link witness buys against the two most recent passive detectors and against a zero-byte counter pair that holds the same information. The witness is an existing primitive: two bytes per packet and two registers per directed link. We replay all three comparators over one shared stream and measure the ledger on an Intel Tofino switch. The witness detects on one epoch of two million packets from a loss rate of 1.5 percent down to one in ten thousand, where the passive cost has grown to ninety-four million packets and then past the budget; its own wall sits at a few times the background loss floor and moves with it. It names the exact directed link, by construction given per-link counts, where the passive detectors return the uplink and downlink pair. A counter pair matches it only when both registers are read within about ten microseconds; at the 2.6 milliseconds we measured it false-alarms on every trial. The advantage fails under a fleet-wide shift and under incast, where a relative passive test is better.

\section{The Receiver Ledger}
\label{sec:ledger}

The receiver ledger is the measured arm of this paper. It is our implementation of the per-link sequence witness that NetSeer~\cite{netseer} and LinkGuardian~\cite{linkguardian} place in the data plane, at reduced width, and we describe it here far enough that the cost, the decision rule, and the measurements of the later sections can be read and reproduced. We claim no design contribution for it. In Figure~\ref{fig:system}, we show the data path from a sending leaf to a receiving leaf and the two places the ledger touches it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_system.pdf}
\caption{The receiver ledger on a sprayed leaf--spine fabric. The sending leaf stamps each packet at egress with a two-byte per-directed-link sequence number. The receiving switch reconstructs the directed link from its own ingress port and the spray field, and keeps two registers per directed link: the last sequence number seen and the number of packets seen. Once per epoch the controller reads both registers, takes the difference of their deltas as the loss on that directed link, compares it with the fleet floor, and applies e-BH to name the faulty links.}

\subsection{Data Plane}

\textbf{Witness stamp.} At the egress of the sending hop, the switch inserts a two-byte header carrying a 16-bit sequence number that it increments per directed sublink, i.e., per directed link and traffic class. The header carries nothing else. An earlier version of the ledger also carried a 16-bit link identifier, which doubled the wire cost. Because the receiving hop already knows its own ingress port, and because the spray field is already on the wire for load balancing, the identifier is redundant for sprayed traffic, and we removed it. Section~\ref{sec:cost} reports what that removal cost in pipeline stages, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reports a correctness cost it introduced for traffic that carries no real spray choice. The stamp is an in-fabric cost only: it is stripped before the packet reaches a host.

\textbf{Link reconstruction.} At the ingress of the receiving hop, a match table keyed on the ingress port and the spray field names the directed link, and a second table appends the traffic class, which the switch classifies fresh for every packet from the packet's size and its differentiated-services code point. Two tables are needed rather than one because the traffic class is a per-packet property, not a per-link one; a single table that baked a fixed class into each link entry would mislabel every packet whose class differed from the entry.

\textbf{Ledger registers.} For each of the $2nk$ directed links, and for each traffic class, the receiving switch keeps two registers: $\mathit{seq}$, the 16-bit sequence number of the last stamped packet seen on that sublink, and $\mathit{obs}$, a 32-bit lifetime count of the stamped packets seen. Both are updated in the data plane on every stamped packet. Over an epoch in which the two registers advance by $\Delta\mathit{seq}$, taken modulo $2^{16}$, and $\Delta\mathit{obs}$, the loss on the sublink is
\begin{equation}
\ell = \Delta\mathit{seq} - \Delta\mathit{obs},
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
because the sender advanced the sequence once per packet it sent and the receiver counted once per packet that arrived. Equation~\eqref{eq:loss} is a transmit-minus-receive difference; the sequence number is not consumed as an ordering, and what the in-band stamp adds over a pair of counters read on two switches is that both terms are read on one switch in one operation (Section~\ref{sec:method} defines the counter-pair arm that isolates this).

\textbf{What the identity does and does not see.} Because the sequence register keeps a frontier rather than a running sum, a late packet manufactures no phantom loss; within one sublink, i.e., one link and one queue, packets are not reordered in the first place, so this property matters only across the epoch boundary. A packet lost after the last survivor of an epoch is counted in the next epoch, when the next survivor advances the frontier. A duplicated packet drives the estimate to $-1$, which the controller reads as a duplicate; a duplicate and a loss in the same epoch on the same sublink cancel to zero, so the signature survives only when duplicates are unaccompanied. A sublink that drops every packet in an epoch advances neither register, and Equation~\eqref{eq:loss} reads zero: total failure is invisible to the ledger by construction and needs a liveness companion, which is why blackholes are used only to validate the testbed in this paper. We verified the frontier, duplicate, and trailing-loss behaviours against the compiled program on the vendor's software model before any hardware measurement (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Decision Rule}
\label{sec:decision}

Once per epoch the controller reads every sublink's registers and tests each sublink on its own evidence against a floor estimated from the rest of the fleet. The rule has four parts, and we state each with the values used in every experiment of this paper.

\textbf{Floor.} The floor $f_t$ for a sublink at epoch $t$ is the leave-one-out loss rate of the fleet's currently healthy sublinks over the last $W=20$ epochs. It excludes the sublink under test and every sublink currently rejected, so that a faulty link cannot inflate the floor used to judge it or its siblings. When the leave-one-out pool carries too little traffic to trust, the epoch is censored for that sublink rather than judged against a fallback constant; a fixed fallback floor was tried and produced false alarms on every epoch of a healthy single-sublink fleet.

\textbf{Evidence.} For a sublink that transmitted $\lambda$ packets and lost $\ell$ of them in epoch $t$, the per-epoch evidence is a convex mixture of likelihood ratios of the binomial loss count under alternatives $p_1 = r f_t$ against the null $p_0 = f_t$, over the ratio grid $r \in \{2, 5, 10, 50, 200\}$:
\begin{equation}
E_t = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{r\in R}\frac{\Pr\!\left[\ell \mid \lambda,\, r f_t\right]}{\Pr\!\left[\ell \mid \lambda,\, f_t\right]}.
\label{eq:evalue}
\end{equation}
A fixed mixture of likelihood ratios is an e-variable, and its running product over epochs is an e-process, because the null $f_t$ is previsible: it is estimated without the sublink's own current-epoch outcome, so the expectation of each factor under the true null is one whatever the null's value that epoch. A censored epoch contributes a factor of exactly one, with no reset and no alpha spending. The wealth $M_t = \prod_{s \le t} E_s$ is reset only when the link is repaired, since a repaired link has a new loss rate by construction.

\textbf{Multiplicity.} The fleet decision at epoch $t$ applies the e-BH procedure of Wang and Ramdas~\cite{ebh} to the vector of wealths $M_t$ over all sublinks at a false discovery rate of $\alpha = 0.05$. Sublinks are ordered by wealth, and the largest $k^\star$ such that the $k^\star$-th largest wealth is at least $|S|/(k^\star \alpha)$ are rejected, where $|S|$ is the number of tested sublinks. e-BH controls the false discovery rate under arbitrary dependence, which matters here because sublinks share spines and the floor. The rejected set is both the detection and the localization: a rejected sublink is a directed link.

\textbf{Reported false positives versus $\alpha$.} The evaluation reports a false-positive rate of 0.00 in every single-fault cell. That is not $\alpha$ driving the rate to zero; it is the e-BH threshold being inert when the faulty link's wealth is large and every healthy link's wealth is near one, which is the regime in which the fleet floor is well estimated. The regime in which the floor is not well estimated is exactly the one in which the rule fails (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}): under a fleet-wide shift the floor moves with the fault, healthy links accumulate wealth against a lagging null, and e-BH rejects nearly all of them.

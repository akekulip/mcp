\section{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

The passive detectors pay nothing on the wire, and the ledger does. This section reports the ledger's cost in the currency each baseline paper uses for its own, from the compiler's resource report on the switch's own software development kit, not from an estimate. Table~\ref{tab:cost} presents it beside the numbers the two baseline papers report for themselves.

\textbf{Wire.} The witness is two bytes per packet. Against the bytes a packet occupies on the wire, including Ethernet framing, preamble, and inter-frame gap, that is 0.14 percent for a 1400-byte payload and 0.02 percent for a 9000-byte jumbo frame, but 0.7 percent for a 256-byte packet and 2.4 percent for a 64-byte one. In a fabric where a non-trivial share of packets are small acknowledgements, the honest figure is that range, not the 1400-byte point. The earlier four-byte version, which also carried a link identifier, cost twice each figure, the same order at 1400 bytes as the $\pm$0.25 percent flow-priority distortion SprayCheck reports for its own prioritized flows~\cite{spraycheck}. The stamp is stripped before host delivery, so the cost is bandwidth on every fabric hop and nothing at the endpoints.

\textbf{Sequence width and read cadence.} The 16-bit sequence wraps every 65\,536 packets per sublink, so Equation~\eqref{eq:loss} is unambiguous only if the controller reads a sublink at least once per wrap. We measured the controller's read loop on the switch: one sublink's two registers take 2.6 ms to read, and a census of all 1024 sublinks takes 350 ms (median of five, range 304 to 358 ms), timed on the switch's own host and so excluding an off-box controller's network round trip. At 25 Gb/s with 1400-byte payloads a port carries about 2.2 million packets per second, and a sublink carrying a whole port wraps in 30 ms. Consequently, with a 350 ms census the two-byte witness can be unambiguous only while a sublink carries under about 190 thousand packets per second, roughly 9 percent of a port at that packet size. The silicon cells of Section~\ref{sec:silicon} ran at 20 thousand packets per second, 3.3 s per wrap, and are unaffected. Above that rate the read loop must be shortened, for example by reading hot sublinks individually, or the sequence widened to 32 bits, which is four bytes of sequence rather than the earlier four-byte header, whose extra bytes were a link identifier and not width. We note that the earlier header therefore does not escape this bound either; both widths wrap at $2^{16}$.

\textbf{State.} Each directed sublink holds a 16-bit sequence register and a 32-bit observed-count register, six bytes, and the program tracks all 1024 sublinks continuously. SprayCheck's own state is under two kilobytes, but it instruments one flow at a time and takes about a minute to rotate to the next~\cite{spraycheck}; FlowPulse's paper does not state a memory figure~\cite{flowpulse}. The trade is continuous per-link coverage of the whole fabric for a fixed per-packet tag, against zero tag for a rotating sample.

\textbf{Pipeline.} Halving the wire cost was not free. Reconstructing the directed link at ingress from the port and the spray field added two narrow tables, and the compiler placed them in a new stage. The program went from 11 to 12 ingress stages, which is the hardware ceiling of the Tofino~1, with 5 egress stages unchanged. It also went from 89 to 91 SRAM blocks and from 15 to 16 TCAM blocks. The compile was byte-for-byte identical on the laptop SDE 9.13.1 and the switch's 9.13.2, with zero errors and the same five warnings on both. The stage cost follows from a choice we made before compiling. To avoid destabilizing logic that other parts of the program rely on, we added narrow tables rather than widen the key of an existing multi-purpose port-role table. Consequently, the program now has no ingress stage headroom, and any future ingress addition must either remove a stage elsewhere or revisit that choice by folding the reconstruction key into the port-role table. We report this as the real cost of the two-byte witness rather than as a footnote, because a reviewer who weights pipeline stages over wire bytes can reasonably prefer the four-byte version.
